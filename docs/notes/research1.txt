Algorithmic Identification and Disambiguation of Sandhi and Samāsa in Sanskrit Morphology
1. Introduction to the Morphophonological Architecture of Sanskrit
The Sanskrit language presents a uniquely complex challenge in the domain of structural linguistics and computational parsing. Unlike analytic languages where word boundaries are clearly demarcated by spaces, classical Sanskrit employs a continuous orthography known as saṃhitā. Within this continuous stream of text, small semantic units (morphemes) and fully inflected words (padas) undergo intense phonetic and morphological fusion1. The ability to accurately segment a continuous character string and identify the underlying grammatical relationships is the foundational task of Sanskrit parsing3.
When analyzing a compounded or coalesced string in Sanskrit, linguists and computational models must determine whether the string represents a phonetic junction between two independent words in a sentence, a single semantic compound formed from multiple nominal stems, or merely a complex simplex word that has undergone internal phonetic shifts. In classical terms, this requires disambiguating between three distinct grammatical phenomena:
Sandhi (Euphonic Combination): The environmentally conditioned phonological transformations that occur at the boundaries of morphemes or fully independent words1.
Samāsa (Compounding): The morphological and semantic fusion of two or more distinct words into a single, compact lexical entity6.
None of the Above (Simplex Derivation): A standalone word derived directly from a verbal root (dhātu) or nominal base (prātipadika) using primary or secondary affixes, existing without external compounding or external sentential phonetic fusion7.
The systematic resolution of these states relies on a highly algorithmic framework formalized by ancient Indian grammarians, most notably Pāṇini in his foundational treatise, the Aṣṭādhyāyī (circa 500 BCE)2. Pāṇini’s explicit rules governing word formation and sentence structure significantly reduce ambiguity, rendering the language exceptionally suited for precision in scientific communication, knowledge representation, and modern artificial intelligence2. This report provides an exhaustive investigation into the mechanical rules, philosophical debates, and modern computational methodologies utilized to accurately identify and differentiate Sandhi and Samāsa.
2. Historical Context and Cross-Linguistic Frameworks
To deeply understand how Sanskrit handles word boundaries, it is necessary to examine the broader historical landscape of Indian grammatical traditions (vyākaraṇa) and observe how Sanskrit differs fundamentally from neighboring linguistic families.
2.1 The Evolution of Sanskrit Grammatical Thought
While Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyāyī remains the cornerstone of Sanskrit linguistics, the analysis of Sandhi and Samāsa was continuously refined by successive generations of grammarians who sought to streamline, philosophize, or pedagogicalize the original algorithms. The historical trajectory of this intellectual tradition reveals the centrality of compounding and phonetic combination in ancient Indian thought9.
Prominent non-Pāṇinian grammars further solidified the structural categorization of the language. For instance, Candragomin discussed the topic of Samāsa extensively in his texts, omitting only the Vedic specificities of Pāṇini to focus on classical usage9. In the first century CE, during the era of the Sātavāhana kings, Śarvavarman composed the Kātantravyākaraṇa (also known as Kalāpa or Kumāra)9. Regarded as the most prominent non-Pāṇinian grammar due to its lucid style, it was designed for those transitioning to Sanskrit from Prakrit languages. Śarvavarman explicitly divided his structural framework into four distinct sections: sandhi (phonetics), nāma (nouns), ākhyāta (verbs), and kṛtya (derivatives), defining Samāsa under the nominal section as the combined meaning of names9.
Centuries later, in the 9th century CE under the Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Amoghavarṣa, the grammarian Abhinavaśākaṭāyana formulated the Śākaṭāyanaśabdānuśāsana9. Consisting of about 3,236 rules organized across four chapters, this treatise grouped grammatical operations under specific heads such as samjñā, niyama, sandhi, and samāsa9. The first chapter dealt exhaustively with Sandhi and word forms, while the second chapter was devoted to Samāsa and taddhita (secondary) suffixes9. Later, the 13th-century Sārasvatavyākaraṇa sought brevity through an aphoristic style based on the Sārasvataprakriyā, showcasing the enduring necessity of formalizing these morphophonological structures9.

While the theoretical model developed for Sanskrit primarily focuses on "information processing," western and some Dravidian models focus heavily on "structure processing"11. Recognizing these structural boundaries is the first step in algorithmic disambiguation.
3. The Phonological Framework: Mechanics of Sandhi
Sandhi (सन्धिः), translating to "combination" or "junction," represents the complex system of conditional phonological change in Sanskrit5. It is a purely phonetic process where sounds are modified to avoid hiatus and ensure euphonic continuity, without altering the inherent semantic definitions of the underlying words6. Understanding the mechanics of Sandhi is an absolute prerequisite for parsing, as these rules obscure the true morphological boundaries of words, making word splitting a highly non-deterministic endeavor1.
3.1 Internal vs. External Sandhi
To ascertain whether a given string contains a compound or is just a simplex word, an analyst must differentiate between the two environmental applications of Sandhi5.
3.1.1 Internal Sandhi
Internal Sandhi applies to the junctures of morphemes within a single word5. This typically occurs during the derivational process when a verbal root (dhātu) or nominal stem (prātipadika) accepts primary (kṛt) or secondary (taddhita) suffixes5. Internal Sandhi is historically and morphologically highly significant, as it dictates the final spelling of isolated words in the lexicon5.
A prominent feature of Internal Sandhi is retroflexion, a process governed by highly complex phonetic configurations that dictate when a dental consonant shifts to a retroflex consonant14. For instance, the retroflexion of the dental  (न्) to the retroflex  (ण्), and the dental  (स्) to the retroflex  (ष्), relies on the presence of specific "closer" and "opener" sounds acting cooperatively14.
The rules of internal retroflexion require either a valid compact configuration (immediate proximity of cooperative sounds) or a valid expanded configuration (where an "in-betweener" group of sounds does not disrupt the cooperative nature of the environment)14. If a single non-cooperative, prohibiting sound intervenes, retroflexion fails14.
Valid Compact Configurations (): ṛna (debt) becomes ṛṇa; karna becomes karṇa; kṛṣna becomes kṛṣṇa14.
Valid Expanded Configurations (): kṛpana (miser) becomes kṛpaṇa; rāmena (by Rama) becomes rāmeṇa; bhūṣana (adornment) becomes bhūṣaṇa14.

If an algorithm identifies a string where only Internal Sandhi has occurred, and the string contains only a single semantic root modified by grammatical affixes, it must classify the string as a simplex word ("None of the above"), as neither external compounding nor sentential boundary fusion has taken place.
3.1.2 External Sandhi
External Sandhi occurs at the boundaries between fully formed words in a sentence or between the constituent members of a Samāsa5. Without mastering External Sandhi, it is functionally impossible to read or understand a classical Sanskrit sentence, as the word boundaries are entirely masked5. It is important to note that while External Sandhi across separate words in a sentence is theoretically optional (dependent on the speaker's breath or pause), it is strictly mandatory between the members of a compound word5.
3.2 Typological Classification of External Sandhi
Sanskrit grammar broadly categorizes External Sandhi into three main types based on the articulatory nature of the colliding phonemes12.

4. The Morphological Framework: Mechanics of Samāsa
While Sandhi is an exercise in acoustic fluid dynamics, Samāsa (समासः) is a profound exercise in semantic engineering. It is the morphological process of concatenating two or more words into a single, compact expression based on their deep semantic relationship6. The primary utility of Samāsa lies in its capacity to generate brevity, elegance, clarity, and immense expressive depth, serving as a hallmark of high Sanskrit literature, philosophical treatises, and poetry6.
4.1 The Architecture of a Word: Derivation and Inflection
To understand compounding, one must first understand how a single word is constructed in Pāṇinian grammar. The ultimate goal of the Pāṇinian derivational system is to yield a pada (a fully inflected word capable of participating in syntactic relationships), as defined by the rule suptiṅantam padam (1.4.14), which states that a word must end in either a nominal suffix (suP) or a verbal suffix (tiṄ)7.
The derivation begins with either a verbal root (dhātu, enumerated in the Dhātupāṭha) or a basic nominal stem (prātipadika)7. Affixes such as kṛt (primary derivatives), taddhita (secondary derivatives), and strī (feminine markers) are added to these bases to form complete stems7. Finally, the inflectional suffixes are added.
For example, generating the sentence "The boy cooks" (kumāraḥ pacati) requires selecting the stem kumāra and the root pac7.
Kumāra receives the suP nominative singular affix sU, which eventually undergoes phonological transformation to yield the visarga in kumāraḥ7.
Pac receives the present tense marker LAT, which is replaced by the tiṄ affix tiP to express the agent (kartṛ), yielding pacati7.
If a string consists merely of a derived stem plus its terminal case endings, it is a simplex word ("None" of Sandhi or Samāsa), even if the internal affixes caused internal phonological shifts.
4.2 The Process of Compounding (Samāsa)
Compounding fundamentally intercepts this derivational process. Structurally, a standard Samāsa involves a pūrvapadam (the anterior word) and an uttarapadam (the posterior word) combining to create a unified meaning6.
When two fully formed padas (e.g., rājñaḥ [king, genitive] and puruṣaḥ [man, nominative]) are brought together to form a compound, the most critical morphological operation occurs: the internal case ending (suP vibhakti) of the pūrvapadam is forcibly elided6. The word rājñaḥ reverts to its raw, uninflected stem base, rājan. This base stem then joins with puruṣaḥ. Because external Sandhi is mandatory inside a compound, phonological rules apply to the juncture, yielding the final integrated compound rājapuruṣaḥ (the king's man)16. The entire newly formed unit then functions as a single nominal base, taking a single, final case ending at its absolute terminus6.
4.3 Classification of Compounds
Classical Sanskrit grammar classifies compounds based on the internal distribution of semantic dominance—specifically, whether the primary meaning (pradhāna) rests with the first word, the second word, both, or an external entity entirely, while the subordinate elements are deemed secondary (upasarjana)7.

4.4 The Analytical Breakdown: Vigraha
To understand the meaning of a Samāsa, students and computational parsers alike must perform a Vigraha—the expansion or analytical breakdown of the compound into its constituent, fully inflected words6. The Vigraha explicitly restores the deleted case endings (vibhakti) to reveal the underlying syntactic relationship6. If the Vigraha of rājapuruṣaḥ yields rājñaḥ puruṣaḥ, the hidden genitive relationship is made manifest.
4.5 The Edge Case: Aluk and Nitya Samāsa
A significant challenge in algorithmic identification is the presence of the Aluk Samāsa. As established, the defining mechanical feature of a compound is the deletion (luk) of the initial word's case ending6. However, in an Aluk Samāsa, the case ending of the first word anomalously refuses to drop20.
For example, caurasyakulam (family of a thief) or dāsyāḥputraḥ (son of a maid) retain their genitive suffixes (-sya and -yāḥ)20. Similarly, the compound yudhiṣṭhira (firm in battle) retains the locative suffix -i on the stem yudh, resulting in yudhi20. Furthermore, the grammarian Kātyāyana formulated a specific vārttika (supplementary rule) stating devānāmpriya iti ca, capturing the word devānāmpriyaḥ (beloved of the gods)—an epithet heavily utilized in the edicts of the Mauryan Emperor Ashoka20. Despite retaining the genitive plural ending devānām, this phrase operates grammatically and semantically as a single compounded entity20.
These Aluk compounds mimic the structure of an external Sandhi string (because both words appear fully inflected), but they are functionally Samāsas because they generate a unified semantic identity20. Similarly, a Nitya Samāsa is a compound that is permanently fixed; it cannot be decomposed into a natural Vigraha using its own constituent words, often because it serves as a strict proper noun or idiomatic identity (saṃjñā)20.
5. Epistemological Disambiguation: Ekārthībhāva vs. Vyapekṣā
The technical distinction between a sentential Sandhi string and a compounded Samāsa string is not merely morphological; it is deeply philosophical. The debate over how to define the boundaries of meaning when words combine forms a massive corpus of Indian intellectual history, primarily housed within Patañjali’s Mahābhāṣya, Bhartṛhari’s Vākyapadīya, and Nāgeśa Bhaṭṭa’s Vaiyākaraṇasiddhāntamañjūṣā (Paramalaghumañjūṣā)17.
5.1 The Principle of Samārthya
The entire section of the Aṣṭādhyāyī dealing with compounds is governed by the metarule (paribhāṣā) 2.1.1: samarthaḥ padavidhiḥ7. This rule dictates that any grammatical operation (vidhi) relating to fully inflected words (padas) is applicable only if those words are samartha (semantically fit, structurally capable, and sharing a mutual expectancy relationship)7.
However, the definition of samārthya became a fierce battleground between the Vaiyākaraṇas (grammarians) and the Naiyāyikas (logicians)22. Patañjali’s Mahābhāṣya explicitly classifies this fitness into two competing paradigms: Vyapekṣā and Ekārthībhāva10.
5.2 Vyapekṣā (Mutual Expectancy)
Vyapekṣā refers to the mutual syntactic dependency between two independent words in a sentence10. Under this view, words present their meanings entirely separately17. They retain their individual denotative power, their distinct case endings, and their primary lexical boundaries. This is theoretically categorized as ajahatsvārtha-vṛtti (a condition where the constituent words do not abandon their original, independent meanings)22.
The Naiyāyikas vehemently argued that only Vyapekṣā exists. They maintained that the individual denotative power of each constituent conveys the primary meaning, and the overall relationship is simply understood through the syntax of the sentence22. Thus, a phrase like rājñaḥ puruṣaḥ (The man of the king) exhibits Vyapekṣā16. The words are separate entities. Even if spoken rapidly such that external Sandhi fuses them phonetically, they remain syntactically independent. A string characterized purely by Vyapekṣā is a Sandhi String, not a Samāsa.
5.3 Ekārthībhāva (Emergence of a Unified Meaning)
Conversely, Ekārthībhāva denotes morphological and semantic unification6. It is the state where the constituents of a compound surrender their separate syntactic independence, drop their distinct case endings, and fuse into an inseparable lexical whole capable of expressing a single integrated meaning7. This is aligned with jahatsvārtha-vṛtti, where the components abandon a degree of their independent meaning to form a new concept22.
The Vaiyākaraṇas (grammarians), led by figures like Patañjali, Kaiyaṭa, and Nāgeśa Bhaṭṭa, firmly rejected the Naiyāyika stance for compounds. They argued that it is necessary to postulate an additional relationship—a unique śakti (power)—in the compound form as a whole, over and above the individual constituents22. Therefore, rājapuruṣaḥ exhibits Ekārthībhāva10. The meanings are not presented separately; they are intrinsically fused17.
Bhartṛhari, the great philosopher of grammar, synthesized these views in his Vākyapadīya, suggesting that the statement of mutual expectancy must be slightly modified24. In expressions of compounding, the constituent words are related to the whole compound only after relating themselves to each other internally, solidifying the reality of Ekārthībhāva as a distinct linguistic phenomenon23. A string characterized by Ekārthībhāva is fundamentally a Samāsa.

Translating ancient philosophy and grammar into a modern diagnostic framework yields a precise, heuristic decision-tree. To determine if a continuous Sanskrit string () is a Sandhi, a Samāsa, or None, an analyst must reverse-engineer both the phonetic and morphological layers.

Scan the string  for potential Sandhi junction points, searching for long vowels (), diphthongs (), semivowel clusters (), or consonant mutations (like anusvāra or retroflexions)5.

It is a single base word. It may contain Internal Sandhi (e.g., retroflexion forming kṛpaṇa), but it contains no external compounding or sentential fusion7.

7. Computational Linguistics: Automating the Disambiguation
The manual application of the aforementioned decision tree is immensely arduous, particularly given the dense, scholastic prose of texts like the Prasthānatrayī (the Upanishads, Brahmasutras, and Bhagavadgita), where continuous Sandhi and long Samāsas completely obscure word bounds4. Consequently, Sanskrit has become a high-value testbed for advanced Natural Language Processing (NLP)4. Modern computational architectures have approached this un-segmentation problem through several paradigms18.
7.1 Rule-Based Finite State Transducers (FSTs)
Because Sandhi transformations are context-sensitive rewrite rules, early and highly successful computational systems modeled them as 2-tape Finite-State Transducers18.
The benchmark for this approach is Gérard Huet’s Sanskrit Heritage Engine (INRIA), built using the ZEN Objective Caml library2. Huet’s sophisticated model provides a finite-state morphological analyzer grounded entirely in Pāṇinian description2. When faced with a string, the Heritage Reader segments it and enlists all possible solutions27. Crucially, it categorizes components using word parameters: it tags stems inside a compound as in initio compositi (iic), while tagging independent nouns as Subst27. By isolating the iic parameter, the Heritage Engine effectively automates the distinction between a Samāsa (stem + word) and a Sandhi string (word + word)25.
Similarly, the Samsaadhanii platform, developed under Prof. Amba Kulkarni at the University of Hyderabad, utilizes extensive rule-based segmenters15. By leveraging traditional kāraka concepts (dependency relationships) and providing discrete tools for Vichchhed and Samāsa generation, Samsaadhanii offers robust parsing15. Other actively maintained projects, such as Vidyut (by the Ambuda group) and sanskrit_parser, push these rule-based heuristics further by constructing dependency graphs to validate the syntactic viability of the proposed splits30.
7.2 Benchmark Corpora
The validation of these tools relies heavily on morphologically annotated datasets. The Digital Corpus of Sanskrit (DCS) offers over 650,000 lexically and morphologically tagged sentences4. Furthermore, researchers have developed specific evaluation benchmarks like SandhiKosh (for Sandhi splitting validation) and PratyayaKosh (for suffix analysis), pushing the field toward verifiable standardizations29.
7.3 Deep Learning and Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) Models
While rule-based FSTs are extremely precise, the combinatorial explosion of non-deterministic splits forces these systems to output overwhelmingly long lists of potential combinations1. To solve this, researchers have pivoted to fully data-driven deep learning methodologies, formulating the segmentation challenge as a Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) prediction task3.
A landmark advancement is the Double-Decoder Recurrent Neural Network (DD-RNN)1. This architecture addresses the fundamental flaw of previous models by explicitly identifying the split location before applying character rules1.
Phase 1 (Location Decoder): The model analyzes the compound and generates a binary vector indicating the exact index where a split should occur (e.g., [0,0,1,0,0,0])1.
Phase 2 (Character Decoder): The location decoder is frozen. Using pre-trained attention weights, the character decoder learns the underlying rules of Sandhi and predicts the morphological recovery of the separated characters (e.g., splitting an o back into a and u)1. Both phases utilize Negative Log-Likelihood as the loss function: , outperforming publicly available tools in accuracy1.
Further innovations include joint end-to-end Transformer-based multi-task architectures utilizing BiLSTM-CRF (Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory with Conditional Random Fields) supervision4. By integrating boundary detection and sequence generation, these models utilize uncertainty-aware coupling to condition decoding steps under boundary ambiguity4. Experimental results for these advanced neural networks are formidable, achieving exact-match boundary location accuracies of 84.39%, exact segmentation accuracy of 79.34%, and character-level accuracy of 87.79%4.
By learning the contextual patterns of Sanskrit morphology, these neural models implicitly learn to distinguish between the grammatical dependency of fully inflected words (Sandhi) and the unified integration of uninflected stems (Samāsa), operating with an accuracy surpassing legacy systems without requiring immense, hard-coded lexical dictionaries3.
8. Conclusion
The disambiguation of a Sanskrit character string into its true grammatical state—be it a Sandhi coalescence, a Samāsa compound, or a simplex word—is a profound exercise in unravelling morphological and syntactic density. This linguistic challenge is rooted in ancient paradigms, delineated by Pāṇini’s derivational algorithms, and fiercely debated in the philosophical discourse of Vyapekṣā (mutual expectancy of distinct words) versus Ekārthībhāva (unified meaning of a compound)2.
Through algorithmic heuristics, one can reliably identify these states by analyzing the inflectional status of the constituent parts following a phonetic split (Vichchhed)3. A sentential Sandhi string consists of independent, fully inflected words (padas); a Samāsa consists of an uninflected stem (prātipadika) fused to a secondary word, stripped of its internal case endings; and a simplex word contains no distinct nominal stems beyond its primary derivational root5. Today, this ancient logic has been flawlessly mapped onto modern computational frameworks, from Gérard Huet's finite-state Heritage Engine to cutting-edge Double-Decoder Neural Networks, demonstrating that the rigorous linguistic mechanics of Sanskrit remain fundamentally mathematically decipherable1.
