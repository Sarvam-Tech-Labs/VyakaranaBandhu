Algorithmic and Computational Methods for the Identification and Resolution of Sandhi and Samasa in Sanskrit
Introduction to Computational Morphology in Sanskrit
The computational analysis of classical Sanskrit presents a unique set of challenges that are largely unparalleled in most other natural language processing (NLP) domains. At the core of this complexity is the language's highly structured and systematic grammar, which was meticulously defined by the ancient grammarian Panini in the Astadhyayi. This foundational text governs word formation, phonological transformation, and semantic compounding through a generative, rule-based framework1. Because Sanskrit was traditionally transmitted orally, its written representation mirrors continuous speech. In this representation, adjacent words and morphemes physically merge and alter one another's phonetic boundaries. This phenomenon necessitates highly sophisticated algorithmic and computational methods to execute "un-segmentation" or word-splitting before any higher-level semantic analysis can occur3.
Determining whether a continuous Sanskrit string represents a phonetic fusion (Sandhi), a semantic compound (Samasa), both concurrently, or neither, is a non-trivial computational problem. Sandhi obscures word boundaries through character coalescence or modification, rendering string splitting non-deterministic and highly ambiguous5. A single sequence of characters might be syntactically broken down in multiple ways, only one of which is semantically valid based on the surrounding contextual syntax7. Conversely, Samasa involves the semantic integration of constituent words where intermediate case endings are typically deleted. This demands algorithmic syntactic parsing and dependency resolution to reconstruct the missing grammatical markers and deduce the relationship between the compounded elements9. The transition in computational linguistics from traditional rule-based finite-state transducers (FSTs) to modern deep learning architectures, such as sequence-to-sequence neural networks and Transformers, represents a major evolutionary leap in how machines parse and understand these complex linguistic structures.
The Paninian Derivational System as an Algorithmic Framework
The suitability of Sanskrit for computational processing lies in its underlying algorithmic foundation. Panini’s grammar functions as an explicit generative mechanism—a set of deterministic rewrite rules that dictate the derivation of words and sentences1. To engineer a system capable of identifying and resolving Sandhi and Samasa, the algorithmic principles defined in the Astadhyayi must first be formally modeled and subsequently inverted.
The Paninian derivational system initiates with a nominal stem (pratipadika) or a verbal root (dhatu) serving as the base-input and terminates with the derivation of a fully formed syntactic word (pada)10. For example, a computational generator tasked with producing the string kumarah pacati ("the boy cooks") must select the inputs kumara (boy) and pac (to cook). The system identifies kumara as a pratipadika and applies the sUP affixes (nominal inflections), selecting sU to express the nominative singular case10. Simultaneously, the system identifies pac as a dhatu and applies the tiN affixes (verbal inflections), selecting tiP to express the third-person singular active voice. Once the internal functional markers (the U in sU and the P in tiP) have executed their grammatical instructions, they are deleted by rule 1.3.9 (tasya lopah), yielding the intermediate string kumara + s and pac + ti. Further phonological rules apply to the terminal s to produce the visarga (h), finalizing the derivation as kumarah pacati10.
Computational linguistics aimed at reading Sanskrit must reverse this exact process. An NLP parser does not generate the string; rather, it receives the continuous string and must strip away the phonological layers to identify the underlying sUP and tiN affixes, thereby recovering the original pratipadika and dhatu. This is significantly complicated by the fact that the boundaries between these padas are almost always distorted by Sandhi or Samasa, requiring algorithms that can detect hidden structural markers.
The Phonological Mechanics and Algorithmic Detection of Sandhi
Sandhi, translating literally to "combination" or "fusion," is the phonological process by which sounds modify each other at morpheme or word boundaries9. Sandhi rules in Sanskrit are deterministic but context-sensitive. Computational linguists classify Sandhi into two distinct operational domains: internal sandhi and external sandhi, each requiring different computational approaches12.
Internal Sandhi and Configuration Scanning
Internal sandhi applies within a word, altering the stems and affixes during the derivation process itself. Computational detection of internal sandhi relies heavily on configuration scanning, where the algorithm evaluates the positional relationship of specific phonemes. A primary example is the retroflexion of the dental nasal n to the retroflex ṇ14. For an algorithm to determine if this internal sandhi has occurred (or must be reversed), it evaluates the characters surrounding the n to verify if they constitute a valid "compact" or "expanded" configuration. In a valid compact configuration, a "closer" sound and an "opener" sound must cooperate without interference14. If these conditions are met, forms like ṛna (debt) or karna become ṛṇa and karṇa. Similarly, expanded configurations allow for specific "in-betweener" sounds to exist between the trigger and the target, allowing ramena to become rameṇa14. If a prohibiting, non-cooperative sound intervenes, retroflexion is algorithmically blocked. Similar complex configuration rules govern the retroflexion of the dental sibilant s to the retroflex ṣ14.
External Sandhi and Combinatorial Ambiguity
External sandhi applies at word boundaries and between the members of a compound12. External sandhi is highly disruptive to tokenization algorithms because it eliminates standard delimiters, merging independent syntactic units. Algorithms must account for three primary external sandhi classes: vowel coalescence (Svara-sandhi), consonant assimilation (Vyanjana-sandhi), and visarga modification (Visarga-sandhi)11.
Computationally, external Sandhi operates as an information-hiding mechanism based on context-sensitive rewrite rules generally formatted as 3. For example, the dīrgha-sandhi rule states that two similar vowels coalesce into their long counterpart, transforming upa + indra into upendra11. More problematic for parsers are cases where vowels change form entirely, such as a + u coalescing into o. A string like paropakarah is generated by combining para and upakarah7. Because the boundary is obscured, a naive splitting algorithm might suggest para + apa + karah, which is syntactically permitted by the rewrite rules but is semantically incoherent in context7. The presence of such multi-split ambiguities dictates that Sandhi resolution cannot be achieved by simple dictionary lookups alone; it requires deep context-aware pattern recognition to evaluate the semantic viability of the resulting tokens.
The Semantic Architecture and Algorithmic Parsing of Samasa
While Sandhi is a phonetic operation, Samasa is a structural and semantic operation. Samasa is the process of compounding two or more words—typically nominals—into a single compact expression, prioritizing brevity, clarity, and expressive depth9. The process combines a prior word (purvapadam) and a latter word (uttarapadam) to yield a newly unified semantic entity9. The algorithm must identify the dominant word, determine the class of the compound (e.g., Avyayibhava, Tatpurusha, Dvandva, or Bahuvrihi), and execute an expansion to recover the original syntax9.
The algorithmic identification of Samasa relies heavily on Panini's governing metarule (paribhasa), samarthah padavidhih (Rule 2.1.1)10. This rule dictates that any grammatical operation involving separate words (padavidhi) can only occur if those words possess samarthya, meaning fitness, capability, or mutual semantic compatibility10. In the Indian grammatical tradition, particularly outlined in Patanjali's Mahabhashya, samarthya is categorized into two distinct paradigms that are highly relevant for computational parsing: vyapeksa (mutual expectancy) and ekarthibhava (emergence of a single integrated meaning)15.
These philosophical distinctions directly map to algorithmic decision trees. In an uncompounded word group (where vyapeksa governs), words maintain their individual syntactic independence, denotative power, and their respective case endings. The Naiyayika philosophical school argued that even in compounds, vyapeksa is sufficient because individual words retain their primary meanings17. However, the Paninian grammarians, whose rules underpin modern computational Sanskrit models, assert that true Samasa requires ekarthibhava. In a Samasa, the constituents lose their independent syntactic status and fuse into a single morphological entity (e.g., rajapurushah instead of rajnah purushah)15.
Consequently, an NLP parser designed to process Samasa must not only split the compound but also perform a vigraha—the analytical expansion of the compound that restores the deleted relational suffixes9. The algorithm must evaluate the semantic head (upasarjana) to restore the missing case endings3. For example, in a Tatpurusha compound, the latter word is structurally dominant, requiring the algorithm to append the missing declension to the prior word to rebuild the syntactic dependency3. Specialized cases, such as aluk samasa—where the internal case ending is exceptionally preserved within the compound, as seen in devanampriyah (beloved of the gods)—require the algorithm to consult specific exception tables or rely on advanced contextual awareness to avoid misclassifying the string as an uncompounded sentence19.
Decision Matrices: Identifying Sandhi, Samasa, Both, or None
A robust computational model must evaluate a given Sanskrit input and categorize its constituent strings into one of four states: possessing Sandhi only, possessing Samasa only, possessing both Sandhi and Samasa concurrently, or possessing neither. This decision matrix requires sequential morphological tagging, phonological rule inversion, and structural dependency parsing.
Identifying "None" (Independent Padas)
An algorithm concludes that a string exhibits neither Sandhi nor Samasa when the input string perfectly matches a fully inflected nominal or verbal form within a validated lexical database (such as the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit) without requiring any phonetic un-segmentation or internal suffix restoration. This state implies the string operates in isolation, carrying its own complete syntactic weight. The system verifies that the terminal characters of the string do not trigger any external Sandhi rewrite rules with the adjacent word, and that the internal structure does not exhibit the characteristics of a bare nominal stem (pratipadika) devoid of its sUP affix.
Identifying "Sandhi Only"
A string is classified as "Sandhi Only" when the algorithm detects a phonetic fusion between two or more independent words that maintain vyapeksa (mutual expectancy) rather than ekarthibhava (fused meaning)17. In this computational state, the underlying words retain their complete morphological markers immediately prior to the application of the phonetic rules. The computational system uses finite-state transducers or neural decoders to split the sequence according to phonological rewrite rules (e.g., separating an o into a and u). The system then verifies that both resulting tokens are fully inflected, independent grammatical units. Once the phonological transformation is successfully reversed, the algorithm assesses whether any suppressed nominal suffixes require restoration. If both words are already syntactically complete padas, the system concludes that no semantic compounding (Samasa) took place, and the fusion was strictly phonetic.
Identifying "Samasa Only"
The classification of "Samasa Only" occurs when an algorithm encounters a string composed of multiple semantic units demonstrating ekarthibhava, but without any phonetic distortion at the internal boundary15. For instance, in the Tatpurusha compound rajapurushah, the boundary between raja and purushah does not trigger any svara or vyanjana coalescence; the characters simply abut one another. However, the case ending of the first word (rajnah, meaning "of the king") has been deleted15. The computational model identifies this state by isolating the first element, recognizing it as a bare stem (pratipadika), and noting the absence of the expected relational morphology (sUP affix). Having detected the absence of Sandhi but the presence of structural fusion, the system must then execute a dependency analysis to determine the semantic relationship and restore the appropriate suffix (performing vigraha) to yield the underlying phrase.
Identifying "Both"
The most computationally intensive classification occurs when a string represents a Samasa wherein the internal juncture simultaneously undergoes external Sandhi. A classic example is nilotpalam (blue lotus), derived from nila + utpalam15. The parser cannot simply look up the stem because the morphological boundary has been phonetically overwritten. First, the algorithm must execute a phonetic un-segmentation to resolve the vowel coalescence (a + u  o)11. Once the phonetic split (nila and utpalam) is achieved, the parser must transition into morphological analysis. It recognizes that the terms form a Karmadharaya (a subtype of Tatpurusha) compound, which requires semantic integration and syntactic expansion to establish that both words exist in the same grammatical case (apposition)9. Modern NLP systems tackle this dual-layered complexity through joint end-to-end architectures that integrate phonetic boundary detection directly with segmented structural generation6.
Rule-Based and Lexicon-Driven Computational Architectures
The earliest and most prominent attempts to automate the analysis of Sanskrit strings relied on rule-based architectures that directly encoded Panini's mathematical sutras into machine logic. These systems utilize extensive lexical databases coupled with finite-state automata to perform deterministic parsing.
Finite-State Transducers and the Sanskrit Heritage Engine
A cornerstone of rule-based Sanskrit computational linguistics is the Sanskrit Heritage Engine, developed by Gérard Huet. This highly sophisticated system models Paninian grammar utilizing finite-state methodologies implemented in the ZEN Objective Caml library2. Because Sandhi transformations are highly systematic, they can be mathematically compiled into 2-tape finite-state transducers (FSTs)3.
In this architecture, the engine stores inflected forms in dictionary modules augmented with morphological tags (such as initial in compound, final in compound, substantive, etc.)4. These dictionaries are subsequently compiled into complex transducers that model the phonological processes of external sandhi. When the Sanskrit Heritage Engine receives an input string, it functions as a shallow parser, traversing the FST to generate a complete candidate solution space of all mathematically possible un-segmentations4. It enlists all potential solutions, differentiating them via phase markers that indicate transition states23. Other notable rule-based systems, such as the UoH (University of Hyderabad) Sandhi tools developed under Amba Kulkarni, the Samsaadhanii platform, and the JNU splitter, operate on similar deterministic principles25. Samsaadhanii notably extends beyond string splitting by incorporating karaka parsing—a traditional dependency grammar focusing on the semantic relationships between words—to help filter syntactically invalid splits generated by the FST26.
Furthermore, researchers have experimented with augmenting FSTs using Optimality Theory (OT) constraint rankings4. By assigning a dominance order to various grammatical and phonetic constraints, OT models attempt to mathematically filter the massive candidate space generated by the FST to output the single most optimal string split4. Some modern Python-based implementations, such as the vidyut project by the Ambuda group and sanskrit_parser by Karthik Madathil, also utilize dependency graphs to map karaka relationships (such as agent, object, instrument, and location) to resolve the ambiguity of merged sentences28.
Limitations of Rule-Based Paradigms
While lexicon-driven FSTs offer high precision for well-documented forms, they face severe scalability and performance bottlenecks when deployed on raw, unstructured texts. The primary issue is the combinatorial explosion of ambiguity. A long Sanskrit string might yield hundreds of grammatically valid splits according to the rewrite rules, placing the burden of final disambiguation on the user or downstream sentential parsers5. Furthermore, rule-based systems are highly susceptible to out-of-vocabulary (OOV) tokens. If a specific morpheme, pratyaya (suffix), or newly coined technical compound is missing from the underlying lexical database, the FST fails to traverse the graph, resulting in an inability to segment the string entirely24.
Deep Learning and Neural Network Paradigms
To overcome the limitations of rigid lexicon-driven models, recent advancements in Sanskrit computational linguistics have reframed Sandhi and Samasa splitting as a sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) prediction task powered by deep learning architectures5. These data-driven techniques eliminate the absolute dependency on exhaustive lexical resources, instead learning the underlying statistical patterns of phonological fusion and semantic boundaries from large, annotated corpora.
The Double-Decoder RNN (seq2(seq)2) Architecture
One of the most significant breakthroughs in automated Sandhi splitting is the Double Decoder Recurrent Neural Network (DD-RNN), or seq2(seq)2 architecture, proposed by Aralikatte, Gantayat, et al.7. Standard deep learning architectures often struggle with Sanskrit strings because a single character modification depends heavily on both the immediate previous and succeeding characters within a broad contextual window8. The DD-RNN mitigates this by directly addressing the core difficulty of Sandhi splitting: identifying the exact location of the split before attempting to predict the restored constituent characters7.
The architecture employs a bi-directional character-level LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) encoder paired with a global attention mechanism and two distinct decoders: a location decoder and a character decoder7. The bi-directional encoder and decoders are typically two layers deep, utilizing 512 LSTM units per layer, with a character embedding size of 128 and a dropout layer applied to prevent overfitting7.
The training process is meticulously phased to ensure accurate un-segmentation:
Phase One (Location Decoding): A compound string is fed into the bi-directional encoder character by character, generating a context vector that passes through the attention layer. The model freezes the character decoder and trains exclusively on the location decoder. This decoder learns to output a binary vector indicating the precise locations of the splits. For example, given the input protsahah, the location decoder generates a vector (e.g., [0,0,1,0,0,0,0,0,0]) pinpointing the fusion boundary between the third and fourth characters7.
Phase Two (Character Decoding): The location decoder is frozen, and the character decoder is activated. Utilizing the pre-trained attention weights that already recognize the split locations, the character decoder is fine-tuned to learn the underlying morphological rules of Sandhi and Samasa. It reconstructs the sequence into its constituent characters, explicitly splitting the o into a and u to yield pra + utsahah7.

Transformer-Based Tokenizers and Subword Segmentation
Building upon the success of recurrent neural networks, researchers have recently explored the efficacy of byte- and character-level Transformer models for Sanskrit segmentation4. Transformers leverage self-attention mechanisms to process sequences in parallel, allowing for deeper context modeling over longer strings compared to sequential LSTMs.
The Transformer-based Linguistically Informed Sanskrit Tokenizer (TransLIST) is a notable architecture designed specifically to address the shortcomings of lexicon-driven approaches when encountering OOV tokens24. TransLIST consists of a module that encodes the character input alongside latent-word information. By injecting latent word boundaries into the self-attention matrices, the model inherently understands the specific Sandhi phenomena occurring at word junctures without requiring a strict dictionary lookup24. Another variant, CharSS (Character-Level Transformer Model for Sanskrit Word Segmentation), further refines the segmentation of subword tokens, recognizing that Indian languages carry deep semantic meaning at the morpheme level24.
Joint Modeling and Boundary-Aware Supervision
In the context of processing long chains of Samasa, identifying hidden boundaries requires recognizing where multiple semantic units merge over an extended sequence. Recent studies advocate for an uncertainty-aware joint modeling framework using structured BiLSTM-CRF (Conditional Random Field) supervision6.
In this architecture, the model performs multi-task learning, concurrently executing boundary detection and segmented sequence generation. The system generates gating signals based on character-level representations to construct a global boundary-aware context6. This context conditions every decoding step, enabling robust performance under boundary ambiguity. When the CRF layer assigns probabilities to sequences of tags, it can explicitly differentiate between a standard character continuation, a Sandhi boundary (phonetic distortion), and a Samasa boundary (structural deletion), making it highly effective for multi-split cases where multiple hidden morphemes must be reconstructed simultaneously6.
Quantitative Evaluation and Benchmarking
The transition from traditional finite-state tools to neural architectures has yielded highly measurable improvements in segmentation accuracy. Evaluating these models requires robust benchmarking datasets. The SandhiKosh corpus provides a comprehensive, manually annotated dataset of Sanskrit Sandhi splittings designed explicitly for validating computational tools25. Similarly, PratyayaKosh serves as a benchmark for suffix analysis, and the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit (DCS) offers a vast collection of lexically and morphologically annotated sentences6. Researchers frequently enrich the DCS dataset with outputs from the Sanskrit Heritage Engine to provide multi-layered morphological depth for training neural networks32.
Performance metrics clearly highlight the superiority of deep learning models in disambiguating non-deterministic Sandhi splits. When comparing the Double Decoder RNN (DD-RNN) against legacy rule-based tools (INRIA, UoH, JNU) and standard RNN models over the SandhiKosh dataset, the multi-stage decoding process of the DD-RNN demonstrates a vast improvement in both location detection and split prediction7.

Synthesis and Future Horizons
The computational identification and resolution of Sanskrit strings into states of Sandhi, Samasa, both, or none require parsing mechanisms capable of simulating Panini's highly integrated morphological and phonological rules. Lexicon-driven finite-state transducers, such as the Sanskrit Heritage Engine and the Samsaadhanii platform, provide deterministic, rule-perfect generation of candidate spaces by compiling phonological rewrite rules into finite automata2. However, the inherently non-deterministic nature of Sanskrit external sandhi, the philosophical requirements of ekarthibhava in compounding, and the widespread use of nested Samasa lead to unmanageable ambiguity and critical susceptibility to out-of-vocabulary failures.
The paradigm shift toward deep learning, specifically sequence-to-sequence modeling, has drastically reshaped the algorithmic approach to Sanskrit segmentation. Architectures like the Double-Decoder RNN and character-level Transformers explicitly model the dual requirement of location identification and morpheme restoration8. By separating the tasks of finding the boundary and learning the phonological transformations, these models successfully learn to identify whether a continuous string merely exhibits phonetic coalescence, hides a deleted case ending indicative of semantic compounding, or requires a simultaneous resolution of both.
The implications of these advancements extend far beyond classical Sanskrit literature. The neural models developed to decode Paninian rules and resolve extreme agglutination have demonstrated significant generalization capabilities, proving effective in related computational tasks such as Chinese word segmentation and processing Japanese Rendaku (sequential voicing)8. As computational linguistics continues to evolve, the fusion of traditional Paninian rule-sets with modern deep learning attention mechanisms promises even greater accuracy. Hybrid models that utilize Transformer architectures informed by latent grammatical tags will likely close the remaining gap in exact-match split accuracy, enabling fully automated, highly precise semantic processing of complex, morphologically rich languages.
